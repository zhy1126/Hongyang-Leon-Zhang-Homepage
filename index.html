About Me

My work sits at the intersection of criminology, human–computer interaction, and computational social science. I pursue interdisciplinary research that uses AI to study—and improve—high-stakes human decision-making, while keeping theory at the center of research design and interpretation.

I hold an MPhil in Criminology from the University of Cambridge and an LL.B. from Zhongnan University of Economics and Law. My research combines experiments, spatial analysis, interpretable machine learning, and LLM-based simulation to study how people make decisions in social and institutional settings.

I welcome conversations about criminology, empirical legal research, and human-centered AI.

Selected Research

Computational criminology · LLM agents

CrimSense: Theory-Guided Crime Simulation

A multi-agent simulation framework grounded in Situational Action Theory. It separates the formation of action alternatives, assessments of situational guardianship, and subsequent choice to examine how personal propensities interact with environments. Current studies compare urban crime patterns, person–environment interactions, and responses to policing, resident cooperation, and environmental improvement.

Ongoing

Human–AI decision-making · Survey experiment

AI Risk Alerts and Police Decision-Making

A preregistered 2 × 2 scenario experiment examining how information density and task type shape police resource allocation, intervention intensity, AI reliance, confidence, and information-search behavior.

Ongoing

Legal AI · Controlled experiment

The Trojan Horse in the Courtroom

Investigating how legal practitioners, law students, and laypeople respond to flawed AI-generated legal advice. The study measures explicit and implicit adoption, confidence, and trust across 222 participants.

Ongoing

Urban crime · Interpretable ML

Predicting Street Crime in a Rapidly Urbanizing Context

A theory-driven analysis of street crime in Shenzhen using XGBoost, SHAP, and partial dependence plots to connect spatial, demographic, and historical predictors with grid-level crime patterns.

Ongoing

Empirical legal studies · NLP

Detecting the Binding Force of Chinese Guiding Cases

Using large language models to detect judicial compliance with—or deviation from—guiding cases, providing a scalable measure of their practical influence beyond formal citation counts.
